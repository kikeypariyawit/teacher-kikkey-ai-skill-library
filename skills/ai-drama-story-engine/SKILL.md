# ai-drama-story-engine

## Purpose

Build the dramatic engine of short-form AI series and episodic vertical films. Own story causality, episode architecture, escalation, setup/payoff, reveal logic, relationship progression, season momentum and cliffhanger meaning.

For vertical social drama, this skill supplies the dramatic material and structural hook; 'vertical-drama-retention-director' owns detailed attention architecture, viewer-question continuity, reveal spacing, visual-reset cadence and retention-data repair.

Do not own final acting direction, camera engineering or model-specific video prompts.

## Use when

Use for:
- AI short films and vertical drama
- season/episode architecture
- rewriting weak, repetitive or sagging episodes
- planning a next episode from approved prior state
- improving motivation, escalation, twists, romance tension, suspense or emotional payoff
- preventing mid-season repetition
- adapting a story to AI-production constraints without flattening drama
- creating a production-ready story handoff for downstream retention/dialogue/performance/cinematic/shot skills

## Research evidence rule

Platform behavior and vertical-drama conventions evolve. Treat timing heuristics as defaults to inspect, not immutable laws. When making a current platform claim, verify current evidence.

## Required inputs

Use the best available combination of:
- premise or existing story
- approved canon / prior episode state
- target runtime and episode count
- genre, tone, language, platform
- character constraints
- audience promise
- production constraints and available generation tools
- prior episode analytics or retention observations when available

If the user already approved characters, relationships, visual identities or prior episodes, treat them as locked canon unless explicitly changed.

## Core story model

For the series define:
- **Audience promise** — the emotional experience viewers return for
- **Core dramatic question** — the unresolved question carrying the season
- **Series engine** — the repeatable conflict generator that can create new episodes without repeating the same scene
- **Character pressure system** — goals, wounds, fears, secrets, leverage, contradictions, loyalties
- **Escalation ladder** — consequences become more costly, intimate, public, dangerous or irreversible
- **Promise/debt ledger** — questions, setups, reveals and emotional promises the story owes the audience
- **Relationship engine** — attraction, trust, resentment, dependency, rivalry, debt, power or misunderstanding evolve through decisions and consequences
- **Novelty budget** — what meaningfully changes across episodes: status, alliance, location function, tactic, information, goal or relationship
- **Production complexity budget** — characters, locations, interaction difficulty, props, VFX and continuity load

## Season architecture

Before drafting a long run, map:
- opening promise block
- first major power shift
- first irreversible commitment
- midpoint redefinition
- collapse / all-is-lost pressure
- final convergence and payoff

Do not hard-code a universal episode count.

### First episodes rule

The first few episodes should collectively establish:
- who the viewer emotionally follows
- what they want
- what blocks them
- why the premise can generate repeated conflict
- the signature emotional reward of the series
- one credible reason to continue beyond the initial shock

Do not spend all major reveals in the opening just to create noise.

### Anti-sag rule

If consecutive episodes repeat the same:
- humiliation
- argument
- rescue
- misunderstanding
- near-kiss
- threat
- failed escape
- exposition pattern

change the dramatic axis instead of merely increasing volume.

Possible axis shifts:
- public → private
- physical → reputational
- romantic → strategic
- external threat → internal betrayal
- victim response → proactive tactic
- secret held → secret weaponized
- enemy pressure → ally pressure
- survival → identity/status

## Workflow

### 1. Canon lock

- inherit prior physical, emotional, relational and information state
- identify unresolved setups, promises and viewer debts
- flag contradictions instead of silently rewriting canon
- identify approved facts that must not be retconned for a twist

### 2. Define the episode job

For each episode state:
- episode question
- one primary dramatic movement
- what changes by the end
- which prior promise is paid
- which new pressure is created
- why this episode cannot be removed without weakening the season

If the episode has no unique job, merge or redesign it.

### 3. Design the structural hook

Open on danger, desire, contradiction, accusation, discovery, unusual action or an emotionally loaded decision when appropriate.

The hook must:
- be understandable enough to create a specific question
- belong to the actual episode
- create or continue dramatic pressure
- avoid context dumping

Hand detailed opening pacing to 'vertical-drama-retention-director'.

### 4. Build the decision chain

Important events must come from:
- character decisions
- pressure
- mistakes
- secrets
- tactics
- prior consequences

Coincidence may trigger a problem but should not solve one.

For every major turn ask:
“What choice caused this, and what new choice does it force?”

### 5. Map scenes

For every scene define:
- scene objective
- conflict/obstacle
- power position at entry
- tactic
- turn/reversal
- new information or changed belief
- emotional shift
- visual storytelling opportunity
- setup/payoff function
- promise/debt effect
- production complexity
- state handed to next scene

### 6. Control exposition

Reveal character and story through:
- action
- subtext
- behavior
- environment
- props
- silence
- reactions
- consequences

Use dialogue for conflict, desire, misdirection, vulnerability, humor or choice rather than explaining visible facts.

### 7. Escalate relationships deliberately

Intensity changes because of:
- proximity
- risk
- disclosure
- jealousy
- protection
- betrayal
- vulnerability
- sacrifice
- changed power

Track relationship state as a sequence of decisions, not labels.

Avoid unsupported emotional jumps.

### 8. Design reveals and twists

A strong twist:
- changes the meaning of prior information
- is supported in hindsight
- forces a new decision
- alters power, goal, relationship or interpretation

Do not use a twist that only says “actually, none of that mattered.”

### 9. Manage the promise/debt ledger

For every open promise record:
- what the viewer expects an answer to
- when it was opened
- whether it is active, partially paid or paid
- latest reasonable payoff window
- what changes when paid

Do not keep stacking unanswered questions until the story becomes fog.

A cliffhanger may defer an answer, but the series must regularly pay meaningful debts.

### 10. End with a consequential cliffhanger

Choose among:
- revelation
- irreversible decision
- arrival/discovery
- betrayal
- danger
- intimate interruption
- identity/status reveal
- moral dilemma
- reversal of power
- unanswered evidence/question

The ending should change the next episode's starting state, not only stop mid-sentence.

Rotate cliffhanger shapes across episodes when repetition becomes predictable.

### 11. Production-aware rewrite

Before finalizing, inspect:
- number of locations
- identity-sensitive two-person interactions
- difficult hand/object contact
- crowd needs
- prop continuity
- costume/state transitions
- VFX
- exact-frame requirements

When two story choices are equally strong, prefer the one that leaves more iteration budget for acting, faces and continuity.

Do not reduce emotional stakes merely to make generation easy.

### 12. Analytics feedback repair

When audience-retention data exists:
- map timestamp drops/spikes to beat IDs
- compare the intended viewer question with what is actually on screen
- generate multiple explanations before rewriting
- distinguish packaging/opening mismatch from story weakness
- preserve beats that consistently hold attention
- repair the smallest causal section first

Do not rewrite canon from one noisy metric.

### 13. Run story QA and repair

- remove redundant beats
- repair motivation gaps
- strengthen weak scene turns
- pay overdue story debts
- remove repeated escalation patterns
- make the final beat earn the next episode
- check runtime feasibility
- check production complexity

## Vertical-retention handoff

Provide to 'vertical-drama-retention-director':
- audience promise
- episode job
- episode question
- structural opening hook
- current promise/debt ledger
- reveal inventory
- scene/beat order
- emotional peaks and quiet beats
- cliffhanger meaning
- facts that must not be spoiled early
- analytics observations when available

Do not force a rigid every-N-seconds formula inside story architecture.

## Final-approve behavior

When the user asks to make or continue an episode end-to-end, do not stop after outlining the story to request approval. Produce the complete story handoff needed by downstream skills.

Only stop for a canon conflict that would invalidate the rest of the episode.

## Output contract

For a full episode handoff include:
- episode premise upgrade
- canon inherited from prior episode
- episode job
- episode question
- structural hook
- character decision chain
- scene-by-scene beats
- scene objectives/tactics/reversals
- setup/payoff map
- promise/debt ledger updates
- relationship progression
- reveal inventory / spoiler constraints
- cliffhanger
- production-complexity notes
- continuity-critical notes
- vertical-retention handoff when applicable

For a season pass also include:
- series engine
- major turning points
- reveal/payoff ladder
- relationship-state ladder
- anti-sag axis changes
- novelty map
- production-complexity strategy

## Final QA

- Opening creates immediate dramatic potential.
- Every scene changes information, power, danger, relationship or emotion.
- Events follow character decisions and prior causes.
- Twists have visible support in hindsight.
- Important audience promises are paid, not endlessly deferred.
- Relationship changes are earned.
- Consecutive episodes do not repeat the same dramatic tactic without a meaningful change.
- Cliffhanger creates one clear next question and changes next-state pressure.
- Episode plausibly fits target runtime.
- Story remains feasible for the stated production method.
- Analytics, when available, inform rather than dictate rewriting.

## Operating rules

- Preserve explicit user constraints over defaults.
- Stable skill rules must not hard-code volatile model versions, prices, dates or project facts.
- Do not invent missing asset approvals, research support or prior episode facts.
- Prefer concrete finished episode architecture over generic advice.
- Preserve approved characters and prior canon unless the user changes them.
- Keep the output easy to hand off to retention, character, dialogue, performance, cinematic, shot-planning, continuity and QA skills.
- If an external action needs an unavailable tool or permission, complete every possible upstream story step and state the blocked action clearly.
