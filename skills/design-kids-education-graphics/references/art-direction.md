# Art direction for educational graphics

## Select the composition by its job

| Message or asset | Useful direction | Design decision |
|---|---|---|
| Real activity or enrollment | Documentary hero | Use one strong authentic moment with a clean text field; use supporting photos only when they add different information. |
| Emotional parent story | Quiet editorial | Give a specific gesture, object, or supplied photo room to carry emotion; keep the hook short. |
| Functional English exchange | Illustrated interaction or real context | Show who speaks and why; point speech tails to the correct speaker. Animals are an option, not a default. |
| Practical learning tip | Object demonstration | Make the objects and action clear enough to understand; avoid decorative pseudo-diagrams. |
| Multiple verified offers | Structured campaign | Group each offer's title, date, price, and conditions; prioritize comparison over decoration. |
| Important phrase or insight | Typographic poster | Make type the hero, with one purposeful visual accent. |

Use these as a repertoire, not templates. A useful alternative changes focal point, composition, or storytelling, not merely background color. Compare against designs actually visible in context; do not claim access to a complete content history.

## Art-direct before adding decoration

Decide the dominant subject, crop, gaze or movement direction, text zone, palette, type character, and one supporting visual motif. Remove ornaments that do not guide attention, communicate meaning, or identify the brand. Premium means controlled hierarchy, convincing materials, good photography, and precise spacing; it does not require beige minimalism.

BEV defaults: warm editorial early learning, outdoor greens, clean cream, a purposeful brighter accent, natural skin tones, and strong friendly sans serif type. Keep colors lively without harsh contrast or muddy green casts. Use straight-edged photo panels by default for BEV unless the newest brief requests otherwise. Keep these as BEV preferences, not restrictions on other brands.

Avoid stacking every familiar education motif into one frame: leaves, stars, clouds, rainbows, sticker badges, wooden signs, and multiple speech bubbles compete for attention. A bright event design may be energetic while still using one dominant visual language.

## Age and audience

| Audience | Visual emphasis |
|---|---|
| Children 3–5 | Concrete familiar objects, obvious action, few competing elements; adult-guided instructions. |
| Children 6–8 | Clear sequence, purposeful play, simple labels and visual choices. |
| Children 9–11 | More agency and challenge; avoid an automatically babyish treatment. |
| Parents or school leaders | Readable benefit, authentic evidence or explanatory image, restrained detail and credible tone. |

Choose only the row relevant to the work. A parent post does not need three separate age versions unless requested or instructionally useful.

## Improve a weak design specifically

- If it feels generic, strengthen the particular child moment, object relationship, crop, or type composition before adding effects.
- If it feels crowded, remove duplicate claims and decorative layers, group related facts, and expand spacing before shrinking type.
- If it feels flat, improve focal scale, light, depth, and color separation while keeping authentic skin tones.
- If it feels too childish, reduce sticker treatments and use stronger typography, clearer alignment, and more authentic detail.
- If it is attractive but unclear, simplify to the one message a parent should remember after a brief glance.
- If a new request resembles recent work, vary the visual structure and useful content; do not sacrifice brand recognition just to look different.
